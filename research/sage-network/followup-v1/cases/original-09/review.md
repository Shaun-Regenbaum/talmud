# Changes required: the core reading holds, but some "Rava married her" links outside the passage claim more than the words say

The story itself is read well. A small girl, the daughter of Rav Hisda, sits in her father's lap. Rava and Rami bar Hama sit before him. He asks her which of them she wants, and she says "both". Rava says "and I'll be last". The Aramaic stops there. The line "and that is what happened: she married Rami first, then Rava" comes from the modern Davidson/Steinsaltz explanation, not from the Talmud. The dossier says so and adds no marriage from this passage. I agree.

The fix to the old automatic pair is right. The machine had cut "Rami bar Hama" down to "Hama" and guessed that Rava sat before Hama. In fact both men sit side by side before Rav Hisda. The graph should say that, and should keep Hama only as Rami's father taken from his name. That father link should carry a note that "bar Hama" may just be a fixed name.

One wording change in the passage: the girl "wants" both men. The text has no word for marrying. The link should be "chooses both", with the marriage sense marked as coming from context. Rava's "I'll be last" doesn't name the other man either; "after Rami" is inferred.

The main problem is in the passages elsewhere in the Talmud. The dossier marks every other "daughter of Rav Hisda" who appears near Rava as locally married to him. That is too uniform:

- In Ketubot 85a she only tells Rava, sitting as judge, that a woman in his court lies under oath. "Her husband" is the English translation's addition. That passage should not give a marriage link at all.
- In Chagigah 5a and Ketubot 39b the hint is weak. In Ketubot 39b, Abaye quotes his foster mother in the same breath, so "she told me" does not by itself mean a wife.
- Ketubot 65a, where Rava goes home and asks her for intimacy, is the strongest evidence. Even there no word says "wife", so it should be called strong context, not explicit.

Shabbat 129a is also over-read. The Aramaic says "they carried her bed after Rava to Pumbedita", after she caught a chill. "Funeral bier" is the translation's choice. So the text does not clearly show her death, and the dossier's open question about whether this is the "same death" as in Berakhot 56a assumes too much. Her husband there is not named. The small story may belong to the unnamed narrator rather than to Rava's own words.

Two smaller points. One commentator (Benayahu) allows both explanations of "last", death or divorce, so he should not be filed under death only. And the two later works on the men's ages do not really disagree, although neither age claim comes from the Talmud.

What stays open: whether the girl in this story is the same woman as Rava's wife elsewhere. Only medieval commentary (Rabbeinu Tam, in Tosafot) makes that link. Whether Rami bar Hama was her first husband also stays open; that rests on Rashi. The later works that repeat both points are copying, not confirming. Manuscripts and the Jerusalem Talmud were not checked. Nothing here approves a historical identity, a merge or a date.
