# Changes required: the core reading holds, but some graph details overreach or are missing

This passage is short. Rav Huna reports a ruling in Rav's name. Someone tells Rav Huna that Rav would have changed his mind had he known a certain older teaching. The text says this was either Rav Nahman or, "some say", Rav Yirmiyah bar Abba. Someone then answers: "He knew it and did not change his mind." The investigation reads all of this correctly. It keeps both possible challengers and does not merge them. It also notes that the reply names no speaker in the original Aramaic. Rav Huna is the natural speaker, but only modern editors write his name in.

The biggest miss comes from the first reading. It counted "the person the new note is written for" as a person. That is a role in a legal rule, not a person. The same goes for "this one" and "the other one" in the Mishnah, and for the witnesses and the court. None of them should become people in the graph.

The investigation is right that the older teaching is attributed to Rabbi Yehuda a few lines later. But that teaching holds two views: Rabbi Yehuda's, and Rabbi Yosi's opposing one. The attribution belongs to the quoted line, not to the whole teaching. The teaching also goes on to "for two reasons they said..." without naming who says it. The investigation left that out. It should go in, with the speaker left open.

In the Mishnah, only Rabbi Yosi's reply says whom it answers ("said to him"). Rabbi Yehuda's objection names no one it is spoken to, so that slot should stay empty. The investigation also labels Rabbi Yehuda's rule "the debtor exchanges the note". The Hebrew says only "he should exchange", and one translation even makes the creditor the subject. The label should keep the Hebrew's openness.

On the Jerusalem Talmud's parallel, the investigation says two commentators identify its "Rabbi Yirmiyah" with Rav Yirmiyah bar Abba. In fact only one does. The other says only "a first-generation Babylonian Rav Jeremiah". The one who says it bases the claim on the Babylonian text itself, so it cannot then be used to back that version. The "son of Abba" link also stays with the Babylonian name. It should not be copied to the other Talmud's Yirmiyah.

On the commentators' debate about what Rav objected to, the picture is more nuanced than the investigation shows. Tosafot accepts the other reading for the final answer. It gives its own reading as what the challenger understood. Three later works follow a single line of reasoning here, so they count as one voice, not three.

What stays open: which of the two challengers spoke, whether the reply is Rav Huna's, who gives the reason that follows, and whether the two Talmuds' Yirmiyahs are the same man. This review approves no identity, no merge, no date and no graph edge.
