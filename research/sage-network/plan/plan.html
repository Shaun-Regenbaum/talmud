How many people are in the Talmud, and how would we know?

A name on a page of Talmud is not a person. One name can belong to several men. One man can appear under several names. We want to work out who is who, when each one lived, and how sure we are, starting from the text and nothing else.

This page does two things. It reviews the work done so far and says plainly what was wrong with it. Then it sets out the plan from scratch: what we read, what we pull out, what we join up, and how we check every step.

One mention, and everything we would record about it

Before any counting, here is one real line, from Arakhin 6a, as two editions print it.

One edition prints
אמר רבי אילא אמר רבי יוחנן לא קשיא
The other prints
א״ר אילא א״ר יוחנן לא קשיא
Names we find
רבי אילא and רבי יוחנן. At this point they are strings, not people.
What joins them
“X said, Y said”: Rabbi Ila is passing on a teaching of Rabbi Yochanan. So the name Ila sits later in time than the name Yochanan. This is the strongest kind of link we have.
The wording
“Said X” with the verb first. That is how the Talmud introduces later sages. Early teachers get “X says”. So both names here are later sages.
What the editions show
One spells out “Rabbi”. The other writes the short form. Every time an editor opened up a short form, the editor decided who was meant.
What it does not say
Which Rabbi Ila. Which Rabbi Yochanan. That the two ever met.
What our app does today
It maps רבי אילא to one entry in our list and shows that man everywhere. I shipped that. It is one of the things this page says to change.

What holds up from the work so far, and what does not

I went back over everything from this project with a harder eye. The short version: the software changes mostly hold. The research numbers mostly do not, yet. They point the right way, but I reported them with more confidence than they had earned.

I read 40 of my own results by hand

The repair was meant to stop a father’s name swallowing the next word. Instead it cut any name whose given name starts with one of seven letters. Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Shimon both became plain “Rabbi”. That hit 129 of 2,479 of the links, about 5%. The fifth error read “Master, what did Shmuel say” as a sage called Mar Mai. None of this was caught by a test, because there are no tests. I found it by reading.

Most “names seen once” are not names

The four most common one-word names in the Talmud are Rav, Rabbi, Rava and Rabbah. My name finder never sees them alone. It glues them to whatever word comes next, so “Rava, following his own view” becomes a sage. This is the biggest open problem in finding names, and it has to be solved before any count of people means anything.

Five rules the mistakes taught us

  1. A name is a string until proven otherwise. Nothing gets attached to a person until the evidence for that person has been weighed.
  2. The Talmud is an edited book, not a record of events. “Said to” does not mean they met. “And some say” is about where a teaching came from. A visit may be a story. Every phrase is a clue with a weight, and the weight has to be measured.
  3. Editions are opinions. Editors opened up short forms and corrected names. Where two editions differ, somebody disagreed about who it was.
  4. Every outside record is a claim to test. Our own list, Sefaria’s tags, the biographical dictionaries. None of them goes into the graph. All of them get scored against it at the end.
  5. No number without a hand check. Every figure I report comes with a sample somebody read, and “we cannot tell” is an allowed answer.

Twenty-eight cases from one tractate is a first look, not a measurement. But it is enough to settle the argument: you were right. Rava against Rabbah and Eliezer against Elazar are different people, and the editions really do disagree about which one spoke. And one of the seven is the exact name I linked to a single man in the live app.

How to use this. A difference between editions is a flag on a spot, not an answer. It says the identity here was open enough that two editors went different ways. Those spots are worth collecting for two reasons. They are exactly where a careful reader would want a warning. And they are a ready-made hard test for anything we build.

How we go through them, stage by stage

  1. Take a dated copy of every edition built

    Each piece is saved with its edition, link, date, licence and a fingerprint, so anyone can re-fetch and confirm they have the same text. Known fault: the same edition with and without vowel marks is counted as two.

  2. Line the editions up with each other not started

    Editions cut the text into pieces differently. We match passage to passage so that a name in one can be compared with the same spot in another.

  3. Find everything that might be a name partly built

    Be generous here and strict later. Open problems, in order of size: the four bare one-word names; short forms such as the two-letter ones that can mean five different rabbis; and the single letters Hebrew glues to the front of words.

  4. Sort each distinct string: a person, or not not started

    A person, a group such as “the Sages”, a place, a legal term, a figure from the Bible, or a title stuck to an ordinary word. This is a judgement, so it goes to the cheap judging model with three real passages each, once per string, not once per mention. A sample is checked by hand.

  5. Find what joins two names partly built

    Count the words around every pair of names, before, between and after. My first three patterns caught 15% of 24,360 pairs, and there are 10,191 different joining phrases. Counting finds them all for free. The judging model then labels the rarer ones.

  6. Record the clues on each single mention one measured

    The wording, the layer of text it sits in, the language of the statement, which body of text it is from, and the title. See the next section.

  7. Build the graph of names prototype

    Each name is a point. Each link has a kind, a direction, a weight, and the passages behind it. The Land of Israel and Babylonia are kept as two separate timelines.

  8. Decide which names are one man, and which one name is several not started

    The output is a set of guesses at real people, each with odds, plus a pile marked “cannot tell”.

  9. Put them in order, then on a calendar prototype

    Order first, from the strong links. Then pin a few people we agree are certain, and let the chains carry the rest. Each person gets odds across the eras, not one label.

  10. Only now open the biographies not started

    For each guessed person, ask of each biography: does its era fit the place the text gives him? Do the teachers it names match who he actually quotes? It may fit, clash, or there may be no biography at all.

  11. Give every mention its odds not started

    Pack up the evidence for a mention and ask the judging model to spread its bets across the guessed people, with “none of these” always on offer.

  12. Publish, and feed it back into the app not started

    The data, the method and the paper. In the app: a rebuilt page index, spelling links that point at lists, and a reader that shows when it is unsure.

The wording clue, which I measured for this review

I took names that have exactly one entry in our list and an era on record, and asked which wording the Bavli uses for them more often. Every one of the eleven misses goes the same way: our list says early teacher, the wording says later sage. And they are exactly the names two men share, one early and one late.

What we join up, and in what order

Two names become one man when they look alike, keep the same company, and never appear in the same sentence. A short name following its full form in the same passage is the commonest case.

One name becomes several men when its mentions fall into separate groups of company, or need two places in the order at once, or use the early wording in some places and the late wording in others.

A biography joins a guessed person only when its era fits where the text puts him and its teachers match who he quotes. If it clashes, that is a finding. If there is none, he is a person we can describe but not name from any reference book, and we say so.

How we check it

  • A check set for names and links. About 300 passages, spread across every body of text and both law and story. A person marks every name, says whether it is a person, and marks what joins each pair. Two people do a shared part, so we know how often careful readers disagree. Every stage is then scored as: of what it found, how much was right; and of what was there, how much it found.
  • A check set for identity. About 500 mentions of the hardest shared names, settled by someone who can read the commentaries. Half is kept locked away and never used while building, the same discipline as the existing pilot folder.
  • Sefaria’s tags as a second opinion, not the answer. Where we disagree, a person reads a sample from both sides: what they tag that the text does not support, and what the text shows that they leave out. Those two error rates are different and mean different things.
  • Checks the data can run on itself. No loops in the order. Nobody named in the Mishnah quoting a later sage. The two timelines kept apart.
  • Read before claiming. Before any number goes in a report, someone reads a random sample of what is behind it. The 40 I read for this page found a bug that every earlier number had passed through.
  • Every bug becomes a test. The guardian who became a rabbi. Rabbi Meir cut down to Rabbi. Mar Mai. Rava glued to the next word. Each one is a line in a test file that fails if it ever comes back.
  • Do the odds mean what they say? When the judging model says 90 percent, it should be right nine times in ten on the locked-away half. We plot that.
  • Write down what we expect first. The main measures are fixed before the full run, so the results cannot quietly choose their own yardstick.

Where this cannot work

About 7% of mentions in the Bavli sit alone, with no other name and no joining word near them. Nothing in the text can say who that is. Names that truly appear once cannot be grouped with anything. For both, the honest outputs are a link to an outside record with its own doubts attached, or “unknown”. How big these groups really are is not yet known, because my count was polluted by strings that are not names.

The order of work, and what each step buys

  1. Fix the known bugs and write the tests me · about a day

    Buys: numbers we can stand behind. Nothing below is worth running on a finder that invents rabbis.

  2. Fetch everything, every edition me · hours of machine time, no cost

    Buys: the full text with fingerprints, and a real count of editions per work instead of my spot checks. The record of what was fetched goes into the repository.

  3. Build the two check sets you, and ideally a scholar · I prepare the sheets

    Buys: every later number becomes checkable. This is the step only people can do, and it blocks every claim of accuracy. It can run alongside steps 4 to 7.

  4. Find the names, then sort person from not me · a few dollars of model calls

    Buys: the first honest count of how many name strings there are.

  5. Find and label what joins them me · a few dollars

    Buys: the other 85 percent of the evidence that my first patterns missed.

  6. Add the clues on each mention me

    Buys: early-or-late for most mentions straight from the wording, before any graph exists.

  7. Build the graph; merge and split me

    Buys: the first answer to the headline question, with its doubts stated.

  8. Order, then eras me, after you pick the fixed points

    Buys: odds across the eras for everyone the text can place, including people with no entry anywhere. It also fills many of the 810 blank eras in our list.

  9. Open the biographies and score them me, then a person on the clashes

    Buys: corrections to the list we ship, and a list of people the reference books do not know.

  10. Odds for every mention me · tens of dollars

    Buys: what the reader actually needs on the page.

  11. Write it up; rebuild the app’s index once both of us

    Buys: the paper, the public data, and a reader that knows what it does not know.

Five things I need you to decide

  1. Who marks the check sets? You alone, or can you bring in someone who learns full time? Two markers is what makes the numbers publishable.
  2. Who are the fixed points? I would start with a dozen nobody argues about, such as Rabbi Akiva, Rabbi Yehuda the Prince, Rav, Shmuel, Rabbi Yochanan, Abaye, Rava and Rav Ashi. You should choose them, not me.
  3. Bavli first, or everything at once? I lean to fetching everything now, since it is free, but proving the method on the Bavli and the Mishnah before widening.
  4. The nine live spelling links. Change them now to point at lists of possible men, or leave them until the study can do better? I lean to now. It is small and it stops the app asserting things we no longer believe.
  5. A spending limit for model calls. The whole plan looks like under a hundred dollars on the cheap judging model. Say the number you are comfortable with and I will stay under it.
